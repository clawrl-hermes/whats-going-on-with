# United States

The United States is the principal challenger to China's Southern African mineral position. Washington's approach combines corridor finance, diplomatic pressure and support for Western mine acquisitions. Its weakness is that it has plenty of frameworks and comparatively little processing capacity, so it can change the route minerals take more easily than it can change who refines them.

## Key Actions

- **May 2026:** The Quad launched a critical minerals framework committing up to $20 billion in public and private capital.
- **June 2026:** The G7 adopted a critical minerals action plan at the Evian summit.
- **July 2026:** Washington backed a rare-earths project in Madagascar and a US-Gulf consortium's purchase of 40% stakes in Glencore's DRC copper-cobalt assets.
- **August 2026:** The MCC expanded its $491 million Zambia compact to support Lobito corridor infrastructure.
- **September 2026:** The US and the DRC created a task force to accelerate mining, energy and processing projects.
- **November 2026:** A joint steering committee reviewed the Dilolo-Sakania rail link, Grand Inga and copper exports.

## Current Position

The US has moved from advocacy to deal-making. It is backing the Lobito Corridor, pushing Western companies to buy into DRC assets and negotiating a minerals partnership with Kinshasa. But the money is slow: the $550 million railway loan had not been disbursed by mid-2026, and the corridor's copper still flows to China. Washington has not solved the processing gap that gives Beijing its leverage.

## Constraints

- Corridor finance is slow and spread across agencies.
- AGOA lapsed in 2025, removing a trade incentive.
- US firms remain reluctant to take majority operational risk in the DRC.
