---
title: The beneficiation bargain
summary: In the third quarter of 2026, Southern African governments turned export bans into processing plants, and Chinese firms proved faster at building them than anyone else.
date: 2026-09-30
---

## The short version

The third quarter of 2026 settled a question that had been open for years: when African governments ban raw mineral exports, Chinese companies build the factories. Zimbabwe's lithium earnings nearly quadrupled, the DRC banned copper and cobalt concentrate exports, and Chinese plants moved from announcements to construction across the region.

The quarter also exposed the limits of that bargain. Processing capacity does not change who owns the mine, who finances the trade or who captures the margin. Western counter-financing grew — a €12 billion EU package for South Africa, a $20 billion Quad framework — but the projects China started were already further along.

## The ban that worked

Zimbabwe was the test case. It banned raw lithium exports in 2024 and spent 2026 extending the policy. In July the government designated 13 more minerals — cobalt, platinum group metals and rare earths among them — for a raw-export ban from the start of 2027.[^1] In the same month, Mutapa Energy Resources confirmed $300 million from Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt and Tsingshan for the Sandawana lithium mine and its processing plant.[^2]

The numbers followed. First-half lithium exports reached $782 million, up 230% year on year.[^3] By the end of September, nine-month lithium sales were $2.16 billion, nearly four times the whole of 2025, driven by a 283% rise in spodumene prices. Lithium sulphate — the processed product — contributed $190 million, shipped from Huayou's Arcadia plant, which had produced the country's first sulphate in April.[^4]

Zimbabwe is now trying to move further up the chain. Officials travelled to China in September to visit the parent companies of Huayou, Sinomine, Chengxin and Sichuan Yahua, with engineers and commercial specialists in the delegation.[^5] The ambition is battery materials and solar panels, not just sulphate. Whether a country with a collapsed railway can build that chain is a different question. The railway to Maputo opened in July, giving lithium a shorter route to the sea.[^6] Sinomine's Bikita plant is due in mid-2027, and announced Chinese investment in lithium and energy has reached $2.8 billion.[^7]

## The DRC's harder test

The DRC went further. A joint ministerial order signed on 29 June and published in August banned copper and cobalt concentrate exports, replacing broad exemptions with one-year strategic waivers granted by the mines minister. The government cited Kamoa-Kakula's smelter — Africa's largest — as proof that domestic processing is now possible.[^8]

The economics are less tidy. The DRC produced 3.2 million tonnes of copper in 2025 and holds 80 million tonnes of reserves, but its mining sector faces a power deficit of about 1 GW and a road and rail network that cannot move the output.[^9] Without that infrastructure, a concentrate ban mostly shifts the bottleneck rather than removing it.

Chinese ownership is the other constraint. Chinese firms hold 70-80% ownership of the largest copper and cobalt mines. A ban that forces processing onshore therefore hands more of the value chain to the same companies that already own the mines.[^10]

## China builds what Africa asks for

The quarter's clearest pattern was Chinese responsiveness. When Tanzania wanted a regional processing hub, Shenghe Resources began building a heavy-minerals separation plant at Pangani, designed to take feedstock from Mozambique, South Africa and Madagascar.[^11] When Namibia wanted value addition, eight agreements signed in Beijing in July committed to local processing of uranium, lithium and rare earths.[^12] When South Africa wanted grid equipment, a delegation led by Electricity Minister Kgosientsho Ramokgopa pitched R2.2 trillion — about $122 billion — of energy investment to Chinese firms, with local manufacturing as the condition.[^13]

The aggregate numbers reflect the shift. Chinese BRI investment in Africa rose 254% in the first half of 2026 to $33.5 billion, with Africa taking 67% of global BRI activity. Much of it went to energy and metals rather than transport.[^14]

## The corridor paradox

The Lobito Corridor was supposed to be the West's answer to Chinese logistics dominance: a 1,600km railway from the Copperbelt to Angola's Atlantic coast, financed by the US, EU and development banks. The quarter produced uncomfortable findings about it.

ECDPM calculated that EU support amounts to about €112 million, not the headline billions, and that the $550 million US rail loan had not been disbursed. Between 80% and 95% of the copper moving on the line is China-bound.[^15] In September, CEDESA argued that China effectively controls a key segment through its influence over contractor Mota-Engil and its ownership of the mines feeding the line.[^16] Days earlier, a Chinese contractor, NERIN Engineering, won a $498 million contract to build a copper tailings plant at Chingola — the planned Zambian terminus of the US-backed corridor.[^17]

The lesson is not that Lobito is pointless. It is that building a railway does not change who owns the cargo. Western financing can shorten the route without changing the destination.

## The West's cheque

Western commitments nevertheless grew. The EU's €12 billion South Africa package, announced in June, ties investment to beneficiation.[^18] The Quad's framework commits up to $20 billion in public and private capital across mining, processing and recycling.[^19] In September, Team Europe launched more than R1 billion of green hydrogen and minerals projects in South Africa.[^20]

The gap is scale and speed. A CRS report in August found Africa supplied 99.9% of China's cobalt imports, 91% of its chromium and 86% of its natural graphite in 2025, and that Chinese FDI in African mining stood at $10.1 billion.[^21] CSIS told Congress that Africa's share of global exploration spending had fallen from 16% to about 10%, even as demand rose.[^22] Western money is arriving as offtake and guarantees; Chinese money arrives as plants.

## What the quarter settled

Three things became clear between July and September. Export bans work in the narrow sense that they force processing investment, but they do not by themselves transfer ownership or margin. Chinese firms are the fastest suppliers of the processing capacity that African governments demand. And the Western alternative is still mostly a financing framework rather than a building programme.

## What to watch

- Does the DRC's concentrate ban survive its own power and rail gap, or do the waivers become the real policy?
- Are Zimbabwe's sulphate plants running before the January 2027 ban on 13 more minerals takes effect?
- Does the Lobito Corridor move any copper that is not ultimately sold to China?
- Will the EU's €12 billion South Africa package disburse at the speed its headline suggests?
- Can India or the Gulf turn mine acquisitions into processing capacity outside China?
