# Russia

Russia is Kazakhstan's most consequential partner and its most immediate constraint. It supplies security through the CSTO, controls the pipeline that carries most Kazakh oil, owns part of the nuclear and space programmes, and shares the world's longest continuous border. The war in Ukraine has made Russia weaker and more demanding at the same time: it needs Kazakhstan for imports and sanctions evasion, and it can still punish any tilt away.

## Key Actions

- **January 2022:** Sent CSTO troops at Tokayev's request to help end the fuel-price protests.
- **May 2026:** Putin's Astana visit produced 15 documents and confirmed record bilateral trade of about $29 billion.
- **July 2026:** Rejected Tokayev's Omsk proposal to freeze the Ukraine war and return to an "Istanbul 2.0" format.
- **July 2026:** Two Kazakh companies were designated in the EU's 21st sanctions package, the first time Astana's firms were named in a Russian circumvention list.
- **August 2026:** Asked Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan to manufacture and maintain arms, a reversal of the post-Soviet supplier relationship.
- **October 2026:** Reaffirmed the Russian oil supply agreement through 2028 as drone strikes cost Kazakhstan about 3.5 million tonnes of CPC output.

## Current Position

Russia's leverage is physical and hard to replace. The CPC carries about 80% of Kazakh crude, Rosatom is building the first nuclear plant at Balkhash, and Russian investment in Kazakhstan rose 45% in the first quarter of 2026. But the relationship has become more transactional: Moscow needs Kazakh territory and imports more than it can offer in return, and Astana has begun refusing some requests, including refined-fuel exports.

## Constraints

- The war has degraded Russia's ability to supply arms, capital and technology.
- Sanctions make Russian banks and firms unreliable partners for Kazakh trade.
- Moscow's coercion pushes Kazakhstan toward the very alternatives — Middle Corridor, BTC, Western AI — it wants to prevent.
