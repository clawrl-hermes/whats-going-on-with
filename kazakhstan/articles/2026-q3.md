## The short version

Kazakhstan spent the third quarter of 2026 doing what it does best: signing deals with everyone. It concluded $15 billion of agreements in China, $19 billion in South Korea and $2 billion in Germany, launched a Chinese-led AI organisation, kept its US AI partnership, and raised Middle Corridor freight by a fifth. It also completed the constitutional transition with a new parliament.

But the quarter exposed the limits of the hedge. Drone strikes on the Caspian Pipeline Consortium cut off most of Kazakhstan's oil exports for days. Washington demanded that Astana choose between its AI bloc and China's. And a White House report named Kazakhstan a conduit for Chinese tariff evasion. The strategy of connecting rather than choosing was becoming harder to sustain.

## The pipeline shock

On 17 July, four tankers were struck near the CPC terminal at Novorossiysk, two of them while loading Kazakh crude. Loadings stopped. The pipeline carries about 80% of Kazakhstan's oil exports, roughly 1.7 million barrels a day, and Tengiz output was halved to 406,000 barrels a day. Total production fell to 1.63 million barrels a day.

The alternatives could not absorb the shortfall. BTC carried about 30,000 barrels a day, the Atyrau-Samara route 220,000 and the China pipeline 400,000. The CPC and KEBCO together account for about 15% of EU crude imports, which is why the strikes mattered far beyond Astana.

Kazakhstan's response was diplomatic and commercial. The Foreign Ministry condemned the attacks as a direct threat to its economic interests. Tokayev used the Omsk forum on 25 July to tell Putin face to face that the Ukraine war should be frozen, proposing an "Istanbul 2.0" format — the most direct public break with Moscow since 2022. Kyiv praised the message; the Kremlin rejected it. Lobbying also secured a Ukrainian commitment to avoid further strikes on CPC infrastructure, though the risk did not disappear.

The longer-term answer is route diversification. BTC volumes through Aktau are targeted at 2.2 million tonnes in 2026, Baku-Supsa is under discussion for 3-5 million tonnes, and refining capacity is to double by 2033. The problem is arithmetic: none of these can replace the CPC at short notice.

## The AI dilemma

Kazakhstan joined the US-led AI Opportunity Partnership in June and became a founding member of China's World AI Cooperation Organisation in July. For most of the year, that looked like classic multi-vectorism. In August, Washington made it a binary.

A State Department note said countries wanting access to Pax Silica AI cooperation must not join WAICO. Kazakhstan had already signed both. The government did not renounce either; instead Kazakh and Uzbek academies announced a joint research AI platform, a quiet pushback against exclusivity.

The stakes are larger than AI. The US is Kazakhstan's third-largest trade partner and a growing investor in critical minerals; China is its largest trading partner and the deepest infrastructure investor. Forcing a choice risks damaging one of them. Andrew D'Anieri of the National Interest argued that if forced, Kazakhstan would pick China because of the depth of BRI ties — which is precisely why the ultimatum is counterproductive.

## The election and the system

On 23 August Kazakhstan held its first Kurultai election under the new constitution. The pro-presidential Adilet party won 71% on 74% turnout. The vote completed the transition from a bicameral parliament to a unicameral one, with a vice-presidency and a strengthened presidency.

Officials presented the change as greater accountability and predictability for investors. Analysts described a more efficient but still dominant-executive system. Tokayev has now removed the remaining Nazarbayev-era structures and revised the Foreign Policy Concept to emphasise citizen protection abroad and countering information influence.

The political consolidation matters economically because it reduces the veto players that had slowed investment decisions. It also concentrates the risk: the multi-vector strategy now depends heavily on one leader's judgment.

## The diversification story

The quarter's strongest economic news was structural. In August, manufacturing output reached 47.1% of industrial production, exceeding mining for the first time. Pharmaceuticals grew 38.5%, fabricated metals 35%, chemicals 26.9% and machinery 22%. Non-oil sectors are now the majority of growth.

Investment is following. FDI reached $20.5 billion in 2025, up 14.4%, with manufacturing investment up 47.4% while mining fell 47%. The government is targeting $25.5 billion in 2026. The Middle Corridor carried 22% more freight in January-August and East-West transit rose 55%; the World Bank identified 16 priority investments worth about $25 billion, over three quarters already under way.

The tech story is real but small. Venture funding reached $209 million in 2025, the ecosystem has about 1,500 startups, and Higgsfield AI became the country's first unicorn. IT services exports reached $1.1 billion. Against a $360 billion economy, these are seeds rather than engines.

## The sanctions squeeze

Western enforcement tightened through the quarter. The EU's 21st package extended third-country targeting to refineries and shadow-fleet support. The US Senate advanced the Sanctioning Russia Act, which would punish countries helping Russia evade sanctions. And the White House's "Great Transshipment Scam" report placed Kazakhstan in the Tier III category of economies used for Chinese tariff evasion.

Each measure is aimed at Russia or China, but each also raises the cost of Kazakhstan's balancing act. The government has responded by keeping visible distance: refusing to export refined products to Russia despite ministry approval, expanding routes, and buying more Russian gas only while flagging the sanctions risk.

## What the quarter settled

Three things became clear. Kazakhstan's geography remains its greatest asset and its greatest vulnerability. The diversification story is genuine but not yet large enough to cushion an oil shock. And the space for multi-vectorism is narrowing as Washington and Beijing demand exclusivity.

The next tests were already visible: whether the CPC stays operable through the winter, whether the US turns its AI demand into a formal condition, and whether the new Kurultai can deliver the investment legislation the government has promised.
