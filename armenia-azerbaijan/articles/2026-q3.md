title: The peace dividend arrives before the peace
summary: Armenia and Azerbaijan spent the third quarter building the trade, transport and institutional scaffolding of a settlement neither has signed, while Russia shifted from coercion to bargaining over the railway.
date: 2026-09-30

## The short version

The Armenia-Azerbaijan peace treaty was not signed in the third quarter of 2026. Yet the quarter's most consequential developments were not diplomatic at all: they were the practical arrangements that assume a settlement is coming. Armenia sent its first exports to Azerbaijan, border delimitation was set to begin in the north, the TRIPP corridor moved from framework to constitutional review, and Yerevan rewrote its foreign policy programme around peace rather than conflict.

That gap — between a treaty that remains unsigned and a relationship that is already being rebuilt — is the defining feature of the quarter. It is also where the risk sits. The infrastructure is moving faster than the politics, and the politics still has a constitutional referendum, an election mandate short of two-thirds, and an Azerbaijani leadership that keeps adding conditions.

## A settlement built in advance

The clearest signal came at the end of August, when Pashinyan and Aliyev met in Bishkek — their first leader-level contact since Armenia's June election. The readouts were near-identical, and within days Armenian goods began crossing into Azerbaijan. By the UN General Assembly in late September, the two foreign ministers had agreed to "promote a positive agenda" in multilateral forums, and Armenian aluminium foil had become the first Armenian export ever shipped to Azerbaijan.

Trade remains small and asymmetric — Azerbaijani petroleum products still dominate the flow — but the direction matters. The border is becoming a commercial relationship before it becomes a normal one. The same logic runs through the transport file: the TRIPP framework bill went to Armenia's Constitutional Court in July, and a development company was created on a 49-year lease, even though construction is now not expected before 2027.

## Armenia's Western turn becomes a programme

In August, Pashinyan declared Armenia's "Fourth Republic" and put a 2026-2031 programme to parliament that, for the first time since 2018, dropped the description of Russia as a "strategic alliance." The programme commits to a peace treaty, diplomatic relations with Turkey, border reopening and 6% annual growth. Pashinyan also signalled that Armenia would begin an EU application process; Zelenskyy endorsed the course, and Moscow warned that Armenia could not sit on two chairs.

Brussels moved in parallel. Von der Leyen's July visit brought autonomous trade measures covering roughly 80% of Armenian exports, which the EU Council approved in September. The early data is striking: in June, Armenian fruit exports split between Uzbekistan, Georgia and Ukraine rather than Russia, and EU-bound exports rose 80% to $516 million in the first half of the year. None of this is membership. Mirzoyan's formulation — that whether Armenia should "marry" the EU is a question for the future — captures a government deliberately keeping the destination open while locking in the economic reorientation.

## Russia changes tools

Moscow spent the quarter cycling through instruments. It set a December deadline for an EU/EAEU referendum, banned Armenian fish, water and soft drinks, and outlined a procedure to suspend Armenia's CSTO voting rights. In September it cut gas supplies for ten days — a shift from trade pressure to energy leverage against a country that drew roughly 82% of its gas from Russia in 2025. Then, in the last week of September, Putin sent Pashinyan a congratulatory Independence Day note, his first public gesture since the election. Coercion had not produced a referendum, so the approach changed.

The railway is now the central bargaining chip. At Bishkek, the two sides agreed to explore selling the South Caucasus Railway concession to a third party acceptable to both. Pashinyan has said Russia's 102nd military base is "not vital" and ordered a comprehensive review of relations covering transport, energy and CSTO membership. Moscow, for its part, has neither offered to invest nor agreed to transfer the concession — the gap between the two positions is the unresolved core of the relationship.

## The corridor race

TRIPP is not the only corridor being built. Turkey and Azerbaijan have committed €2.4 billion to the Kars-Dilucu railway, which bypasses Armenia entirely; analysts warned in September that the Middle Corridor could outgrow the peace process and leave Yerevan with a route that is strategically optional for everyone else. TRIPP itself is modest by comparison — around $400 million with no private operator yet — and Yerevan has ruled out any foreign military or security role on Armenian soil, narrowing what the project can offer Washington.

The corridor's value to Armenia, then, is less about transit revenue than about lock-in: making the country too useful to the region's connectivity to be left out of it. That is a plausible strategy, but it depends on partners who have alternatives.

## What to watch

- Whether the constitutional draft, finalized in August but still unpublished, is put to a referendum — and whether Pashinyan can win one without a two-thirds majority.
- Whether Aliyev's additional demands on enclaves and resettlement narrow or expand once a treaty text is formally on the table.
- Whether the railway working group produces Russian investment or becomes a holding pattern.
- Whether TRIPP's 2027 construction target holds, and whether the Kars-Dilucu bypass draws away the freight TRIPP needs.
- Whether Russia returns to coercion as winter gas negotiations approach.
