---
title: The peace dividend arrives before the peace
summary: Armenia and Azerbaijan spent the third quarter building the trade, transport and institutional scaffolding of a settlement neither has signed, while Russia shifted from coercion to bargaining over the railway.
date: 2026-09-30
---

## The short version

The Armenia-Azerbaijan peace treaty was not signed in the third quarter of 2026. The quarter's most consequential developments were practical arrangements that assume one is coming: Armenia shipped its first exports to Azerbaijan,[^1] the sides agreed to restart border delimitation from the north,[^2] the TRIPP framework went to the Constitutional Court,[^3] and Yerevan adopted a 2026-2031 programme built around peace.[^4]

The gap between the unsigned treaty and the rebuilding relationship is the quarter's defining feature. Baku's official position is that the conflict is over,[^5] while Yerevan is building facts on the ground that would make a treaty harder to reverse. Both sides are acting as if the politics will catch up.

## A settlement built in advance

Pashinyan and Aliyev met in Bishkek on 31 August, their first leader-level contact since June's election.[^1] Mirzoyan said Armenian exports would begin "in the next few days", and by 24 September Armenia had shipped aluminium foil — its first-ever export to Azerbaijan.[^6] Until then trade had been one-way: Azerbaijan sold $17.4 million of goods, mostly petroleum, in January-July, while Armenian exports were zero.[^7] The volumes are modest, but they make trade two-way for the first time.

The transport file follows the same logic. The government approved the TRIPP framework bill on 16 July and sent it to the Constitutional Court; the law creates a development company holding a 49-year lease.[^3] Bayramov said in September that construction will not start until 2027.[^8]

## Armenia's Western turn becomes a programme

On 20 August Pashinyan declared the "Fourth Republic" and "Real Armenia" ideology, and on 24 August presented the 2026-2031 programme to parliament.[^4] The document drops the description of Russia as a "strategic alliance" in favour of a "transforming and deepening" partnership — what OC Media called a formal downgrade in diplomatic language.[^9] Its priorities are institutionalising peace with Azerbaijan, TRIPP, visa liberalisation with the EU, 6% annual growth and SCO membership.[^10]

Pashinyan said Armenia would begin an EU application process. Zelenskyy endorsed the course, and a Russian Duma deputy warned that Armenia could not "sit on two chairs".[^11]

Brussels moved in parallel. Von der Leyen's 2 July visit announced autonomous trade measures covering roughly 80% of Armenian exports, and the EU Council approved two-year duty-free access on 5 September.[^12][^13] The shift in trade geography predates the measures: Armenian exports to the EU rose more than 80% to $516 million in January-June, according to government data.[^14] The Council measure is temporary trade access, not a membership track.

## Russia changes tools

Moscow spent the quarter cycling through instruments. Deputy Foreign Minister Mikhail Galuzin said on 11 August that Armenia must decide by December whether to hold a referendum on EU or EAEU membership.[^15] Rospotrebnadzor blocked the sale of 70,000 bottles of Armenian water and soft drinks.[^16] Russia outlined the Article 25 procedure for suspending Armenia's CSTO voting rights at a Moscow summit expected in November; the decision requires consensus.[^17]

On 15 September Gazprom suspended gas supplies for ten days, calling it scheduled maintenance. Russia supplied about 82% of Armenia's gas imports in 2025.[^18] Then, on 21 September, Putin sent Pashinyan a congratulatory Independence Day note — his first public gesture since the election. Eurasianet read the greetings as a shift from coercion to a charm offensive after intimidation failed to move Pashinyan.[^19]

The railway concession has become the main item of bargaining. At the same Bishkek summit, Pashinyan and Putin agreed to explore selling the South Caucasus Railway concession to a third party acceptable to both; Kazakhstan has been mentioned as a possible buyer, and MassisPost reported that Russia strongly opposes the idea.[^20][^21] Pashinyan has called Russia's 102nd military base "not vital" and confirmed a comprehensive "inventory" of relations covering transport and energy.[^22] In July, JAMnews reported that Russia had not signalled readiness to proceed with railway works but would not give up the concession.[^23]

## The corridor race

TRIPP is not the only corridor being built. Turkey and Azerbaijan have committed €2.4 billion to the Kars-Dilucu railway, which bypasses Armenia entirely, with construction planned for 2026-2030; TRIPP itself is put at around $400 million with no private operator named. Modern Diplomacy's base case, at 55%, is that the corridor outgrows the peace process and that Armenia ends up missing it rather than blocking it.[^24]

Armenia's bet is different. The corridor's value is less about transit revenue than about lock-in: making the country too useful to the region's connectivity to be left out. That is a plausible strategy, but it depends on partners who have alternatives — and on the treaty that would make the route permanent.

Yerevan has ruled out any foreign military or security role. A Civil Contract MP said the framework keeps the border, customs and security under exclusive Armenian jurisdiction.[^25]

## What to watch

- Whether the constitutional draft, finalised in August, is published and put to a referendum — and whether Pashinyan can win one without a two-thirds majority.[^26]
- Whether Aliyev's additional demands on enclaves and resettlement narrow or expand once a treaty text is formally on the table.
- Whether the railway working group produces Russian investment or becomes a holding pattern.
- Whether TRIPP's 2027 construction target holds, and whether the Kars-Dilucu bypass draws away the freight TRIPP needs.
- Whether Russia returns to coercion as winter gas negotiations approach.
