---
title: The peace dividend arrives before the peace
summary: Armenia and Azerbaijan spent the third quarter building the trade, transport and institutional scaffolding of a settlement neither has signed, while Russia shifted from coercion to bargaining over the railway.
date: 2026-09-30
---

## The short version

The Armenia-Azerbaijan peace treaty was not signed in the third quarter of 2026. Yet the quarter's most consequential developments were practical arrangements that assume a settlement is coming: Armenia shipped its first exports to Azerbaijan,[^1] the sides agreed to restart border delimitation from the north,[^2] the TRIPP framework went to the Constitutional Court,[^3] and Yerevan put a 2026-2031 programme to parliament that treats peace as the organising principle.[^4]

That gap — an unsigned treaty beside a relationship already being rebuilt — is where Modern Diplomacy located the risk. In late September it argued that the process had been stalled for 18 months and that the Middle Corridor could outgrow the peace process rather than be driven by it.[^5]

## A settlement built in advance

Pashinyan and Aliyev met in Bishkek on 31 August, their first leader-level contact since June's election.[^1] Mirzoyan said Armenian exports would begin "in the next few days", and by 24 September Armenia had shipped aluminium foil — its first-ever export to Azerbaijan.[^6] Until then trade had been one-way: Azerbaijan sold $17.4 million of goods, mostly petroleum, in January-July, while Armenian exports were zero.[^7] The volumes are modest, but they make trade two-way for the first time.

The transport file follows the same logic. The government approved the TRIPP framework bill on 16 July and sent it to the Constitutional Court; the law creates a development company holding a 49-year lease.[^3] Bayramov said in September that construction will not start until 2027.[^8]

## Armenia's Western turn becomes a programme

On 20 August Pashinyan declared the "Fourth Republic" and "Real Armenia" ideology, and on 24 August presented the 2026-2031 programme to parliament.[^9] The document drops the description of Russia as a "strategic alliance" in favour of a "transforming and deepening" partnership — what OC Media called a formal downgrade in diplomatic language.[^10] Its priorities are institutionalising peace with Azerbaijan, TRIPP, visa liberalisation with the EU, 6% annual growth and SCO membership.[^11]

Pashinyan said Armenia would begin an EU application process. Zelenskyy endorsed the course, and a Russian Duma deputy warned that Armenia could not "sit on two chairs".[^12]

Brussels moved in parallel. Von der Leyen's 2 July visit announced autonomous trade measures covering roughly 80% of Armenian exports, and the EU Council approved two-year duty-free access on 5 September.[^13][^14] The economic shift is visible in the trade data: Russia's share of Armenia's turnover fell from 35.1% to 28% in January-May, and Armenia exported 200,000 flowers to the UAE.[^15][^16] The Council measure is temporary trade access, not a membership track.

## Russia changes tools

Moscow spent the quarter cycling through instruments. Deputy Prime Minister Galuzin said on 11 August that Armenia must decide by December whether to hold a referendum on EU or EAEU membership.[^17] Rospotrebnadzor blocked the sale of 70,000 bottles of Armenian water and soft drinks.[^18] Russia outlined the Article 25 procedure for suspending Armenia's CSTO voting rights at a Moscow summit expected in November; the decision requires consensus.[^19]

On 15-25 September Gazprom suspended gas supplies, calling it scheduled maintenance. Russia supplied about 82% of Armenia's gas imports in 2025.[^20] Then, on 21 September, Putin sent Pashinyan a congratulatory Independence Day note — his first public gesture since the election. Eurasianet read the greetings as a shift from coercion to a charm offensive after intimidation failed to move Pashinyan.[^21]

The railway concession has become the main item of bargaining. At his September meeting with Putin in Bishkek, Pashinyan agreed to explore selling the South Caucasus Railway concession to a third party acceptable to both; Kazakhstan has been mentioned as a possible buyer, and Russia opposes the idea.[^22][^27] Pashinyan has called Russia's 102nd military base "not vital" and confirmed a comprehensive "inventory" of relations covering transport and energy.[^23] JAMnews reported that Russia has not signalled readiness to invest in the railway but will not give up the concession.[^24]

## The corridor race

TRIPP is not the only corridor being built. Turkey and Azerbaijan have committed €2.4 billion to the Kars-Dilucu railway, which bypasses Armenia entirely, with construction planned for 2026-2030. TRIPP itself is put at around $400 million with no private operator named.[^5] Modern Diplomacy's base case, at 55%, is that the corridor outgrows the peace process and that Armenia ends up missing it rather than blocking it.[^5]

Yerevan has ruled out any foreign military or security role. A Civil Contract MP said the framework keeps the border, customs and security under exclusive Armenian jurisdiction.[^25]

## What to watch

- Whether the constitutional draft, finalised in August but still unpublished, is put to a referendum — and whether Pashinyan can win one without a two-thirds majority.[^26]
- Whether Aliyev's additional demands on enclaves and resettlement narrow or expand once a treaty text is formally on the table.
- Whether the railway working group produces Russian investment or becomes a holding pattern.
- Whether TRIPP's 2027 construction target holds, and whether the Kars-Dilucu bypass draws away the freight TRIPP needs.
- Whether Russia returns to coercion as winter gas negotiations approach.
