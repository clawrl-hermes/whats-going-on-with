# Border Delimitation

## Overview
Armenia and Azerbaijan share roughly 1,000 km of border, but for three decades it existed only as a ceasefire line. Delimitation is the attempt to convert it into a surveyed legal boundary based on the 1991 Alma-Ata Declaration, which both sides accept as the basis for mutual recognition. Only about 12 km had been delimited by October 2026.

## Why It Is Difficult
- **No agreed map.** The Soviet border was an administrative line, and the two sides dispute which maps and which scale are authoritative.
- **Armies face each other.** Positions taken during the 1990s and 2020 wars sit on both sides of the legal line, so any correction moves real troops.
- **Enclaves and exclaves.** Each side holds small territories inside the other, and neither wants to trade them without guarantees.
- **Strategic ground.** Villages sit on the roads linking Yerevan to Georgia and Iran, so minor map changes carry outsized military value.

## The Enclaves
- **Azerbaijani exclaves in Armenia:** Karki (containing the village of Tigranashen), Yukhari Askipara, Barkhudarli and Sofulu. The last three have been under Armenian control since 1992.
- **Armenian exclave in Azerbaijan:** Artsvashen (Bashkend), under Azerbaijani control since 1992.
- In October 2026 Pashinyan proposed exchanging Karki, Barkhudarli and Yukhari Askipara for Artsvashen under a "no enclaves or exclaves" formula. Mirzoyan said the enclaves "do not pose an obstacle" to peace; no swap has been agreed.

## The Process
- **2022:** The two governments set up delimitation commissions, chaired by Deputy PM Mher Grigoryan and his Azerbaijani counterpart.
- **April 2024:** Armenia returned four Tavush villages — Baghanis Ayrum, Voskepar, Kirants and Berkaber — the first concrete demarcation, and the trigger for prolonged protests.
- **September 2026:** The sides agreed to resume from the northern tripoint where Armenia, Azerbaijan and Georgia meet, then work south.
- **October 2026:** Pashinyan named 2031 as his target for signing and ratifying a treaty, with delimitation continuing alongside.

## Current Status
Work is slow and politically explosive. Rumours of concessions near Jermuk and Berdavan have produced official denials and opposition protests, and Baku links progress to constitutional change while Yerevan treats delimitation as proof the border can be settled without a treaty. The TRIPP corridor and the "Western Azerbaijan" claims run through the same terrain, which makes the map the peace process's most sensitive file.
