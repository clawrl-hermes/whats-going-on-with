# The Constitutional Question

## Overview
Azerbaijan says a peace treaty cannot be signed until Armenia removes the territorial claims it reads into its constitution. Yerevan accepts that change is needed but cannot deliver it quickly: amendments require a Constitutional Court review, a two-thirds parliamentary vote and a referendum.

## What Azerbaijan Demands
- Baku's core objection is the preamble's reference to the 1990 Declaration of Independence, which it says implies a claim to Nagorno-Karabakh and provides the legal basis for "territorial demands".
- Hikmet Hajiyev has called constitutional change the "only remaining step" and said the treaty becomes reality once it is done. Turkey ties the border opening to the same sequencing.
- Aliyev has added further conditions around it — enclaves, border adjustments and the "Western Azerbaijan" campaign — which Yerevan treats as evidence that the goalposts keep moving.

## Why It Is Hard
- **Arithmetic:** Civil Contract won 49.8% in June 2026 and was six votes short of two-thirds when the new parliament opened. The government has floated snap elections to close the gap.
- **Legitimacy:** the draft was finalised by the Justice Ministry and reviewed inside the ruling party before publication, which critics say weakens its mandate.
- **Opposition:** the parties call it "Aliyev's Constitution" and have announced a "pan-national consolidation" against it, while every major leader is in pretrial detention.
- **Institutions:** the Constitutional Court must review the amendments, and its new president, Vladimir Vardanyan, was appointed in November 2026 after all its judges were installed under Pashinyan.

## Where It Stands
The draft is expected to be published by end-2026. The parliamentary vote and the referendum that follows have, in the assessment of the Caspian Policy Center, "no clear path to success". Mirzoyan insists Baku does not formally table the constitution as a negotiating demand, but Baku's public position and Turkey's sequencing make it the practical precondition.

## What Happens If It Fails
Without the amendments there is no treaty, no Turkish border opening, and a prolonged "no peace, no war" in which trade and de facto peace continue without legal settlement. That is the outcome analysts warn the current stalemate is already producing.
