# Bolivia's Lithium Geopolitics

## Overview

Bolivia sits in the Lithium Triangle and holds the world's largest single-basin deposit at Salar de Uyuni, with about 23 million tonnes of identified lithium. It produces less than 1% of global supply. The gap between reserve and output is the central fact of Bolivian resource politics: the country has the asset that could replace its dying gas revenue, but not the legal, technical or political capacity to extract it at scale.

## The Resource

USGS credits Uyuni with 23 million tonnes but assigns Bolivia no reserve classification, because no extraction method has been commercially proven there. The state company YLB produced about 2,000 tonnes in 2024 against a 15,000-tonne nameplate at its flagship plant. High magnesium ratios make conventional evaporation difficult, and direct lithium extraction has reached pilot stage but not industrial scale.

## Why It Is Stuck

Three locks hold the sector shut. The 2009 constitution requires foreign companies to work through YLB, and changing that needs a two-thirds majority Paz does not have. The $2 billion in Chinese and Russian contracts signed under Morales remain unratified, challenged in court by Indigenous communities and under review by the Constitutional Tribunal. And the state lacks the capital and technical staff to develop the salars alone. Paz has courted Western companies and signed a critical-minerals memorandum with Washington, but a new lithium law has not passed.

## The Geopolitical Contest

China and Russia signed the first contracts, through CBC/CATL and Uranium One, and Beijing has linked lithium to its broader credit and technology relationship. Washington is trying to displace them: Bolivia joined the Shield of the Americas, and the US signed a minerals memorandum in April 2026. The competition is constrained by the same legislative blockage, so neither side can convert political favour into production.

## Current Status

The contracts are still in limbo, Chinese refining-technology export controls took effect in November 2026, and Chile's Codelco-SQM ramp-up is locking in Western offtake next door. The most likely outcome is a two-speed Lithium Triangle in which Chile and Argentina supply the world while Bolivia remains a resource-rich spectator. That changes only if a government can ratify or replace the contracts and pass a lithium law.
