# South America's Rightward Shift

## Overview

South America has swung right since 2023, and Bolivia is part of the pattern. Argentina, Chile, Peru, Colombia, Ecuador and Bolivia all elected conservative or right-leaning governments, most of them promising austerity and hard security policies. The wave is less an ideological conversion than a reaction to inflation, crime and the exhaustion of the left's economic model. Its durability is untested.

## The Wave

The common drivers are economic. Post-pandemic inflation, collapsing commodity revenue and rising organised crime punished incumbents across the continent. The style is borrowed from El Salvador and the United States: confrontational leaders, rapid security crackdowns, minimal deference to due process. Peru's Keiko Fujimori, Colombia's Abelardo de la Espriella, Chile's José Antonio Kast and Argentina's Javier Milei all campaigned on variants of the same offer. Bolivia's Rodrigo Paz is the least ideological of them, a centrist who governs with a right-wing coalition.

## Bolivia's Place

Bolivia fits the wave economically but not politically. Paz ran as a centrist and won a runoff against Jorge Quiroga, the traditional right's candidate, then assembled a coalition of six centre-right blocs. His programme is an IMF stabilisation, not a culture-war agenda. The regional alignment nonetheless matters: it gave him the Shield of the Americas, US security cooperation and, in October 2026, the first Chilean state visit in 71 years. Right-wing solidarity has become a foreign-policy asset.

## The Limits

The wave is broad but shallow. Most winners took office with narrow margins and weak legislative mandates, and their austerity programmes are already unpopular. Brazil was the decisive test: Lula's runoff victory over Flávio Bolsonaro in October 2026 denied the right its largest prize and capped the wave's reach. Analysts note that the region's right is winning elections faster than it is building durable parties.

## Current Status

The shift has peaked rather than consolidated. Bolivia's government depends on the same fragile coalition that the regional wave helped elect, and its economic programme is generating the discontent that could reverse it. The right's advantage is that the left remains fragmented; its vulnerability is that it has not delivered growth or security yet.
