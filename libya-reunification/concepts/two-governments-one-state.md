# Two Governments, One State

## Overview

Libya has one internationally recognised state and two competing administrations. The Tripoli-based Government of National Unity is treated by the UN as the interim executive; the Benghazi-based Government of National Stability administers the east and much of the south with the House of Representatives and Khalifa Haftar's Libyan National Army behind it. The split is not a front line. It is a duplicated state, with parallel ministries, payrolls, security forces and foreign patrons.

## How the Split Works

The divide dates to 2014, when the elected House of Representatives moved to Tobruk after armed groups took Tripoli and a rival parliament claimed authority. The 2015 Skhirat agreement created a Presidential Council and a unity government, but the east never accepted its authority. Since then, each side has built the institutions it needs: the west has the recognised ministries and the Central Bank; the east has the largest oil terminals, the strongest army and its own government.

## Why Revenue Is the Real Contest

Both administrations are funded from the same source. Oil is sold by the National Oil Corporation and the proceeds flow through the Central Bank of Libya, which pays salaries across the country. Control of the bank, the exchange rate and the budget is therefore more valuable than control of territory. The April 2026 unified budget agreement was an attempt to manage that shared dependency; the battle over the bank's leadership in late 2026 shows how fragile it remains.

## What Unification Would Require

A single executive, a merged security apparatus, one budget and an election that both sides accept. Each is a zero-sum question for someone: the eastern command would have to give up independent military authority, western armed groups would have to accept integration, and both political classes would have to risk elections they might lose.

## Current Status

The 4+4 agreement created a timetable but not a government. The High Council of State rejects its outcomes, the US and Turkish tracks are negotiating a leadership bargain outside it, and the central bank and oil disruptions are testing whether either side can afford to keep the split running.
