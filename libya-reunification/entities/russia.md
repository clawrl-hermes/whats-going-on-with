# Russia

Russia has backed Khalifa Haftar since the mid-2010s, first through Wagner and now through the Africa Corps, using eastern Libya as a logistics corridor to the Sahel and as a foothold in the Mediterranean. It is not a mediator: it is a patron with a veto at the Security Council, and its interests are the hardest to reconcile with a unified state that would have to choose its foreign alignments.

## Key Actions

- **2017-2020:** Deployed Wagner personnel and equipment to support Haftar's forces, including during the Tripoli offensive.
- **May 2025:** Putin received Haftar in Moscow, consolidating the relationship at head-of-state level.
- **August 2026:** Saddam Haftar met Putin in Moscow and committed to deeper cooperation "across various fields".
- **October 2026:** Joined the unanimous Security Council renewal of UNSMIL's mandate, preserving the diplomatic track while maintaining its military presence.
- **November 2026:** After Ukrainian strikes on a Russian vessel in Benghazi, Russian experts arrived to investigate and supply routes shifted toward Al-Khadim and Al-Jufra.

## Current Position

Russia's priority is to keep its bases and logistics access, not to control Libya. It has little interest in a strong unified government that might ask it to leave, but it also does not want to be sidelined by a US-Turkey deal. Its veto makes it a necessary party to any Security Council endorsement, and its arms and trainers give Haftar an alternative to Western support. The Ukrainian strikes have made its presence more costly without changing its strategic value.

## Constraints

- The war in Ukraine and the loss of its Syrian bases have increased the importance of Libya, but also stretched its logistics.
- Its presence depends on Haftar; a unified government could legitimately demand withdrawal.
- Western sanctions and the UN arms embargo limit its ability to operate openly.
