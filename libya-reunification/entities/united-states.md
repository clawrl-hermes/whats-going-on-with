# United States

The United States has oscillated between disengagement and episodic diplomacy in Libya. Under the Trump administration it has become the most active outside broker, through Massad Boulos's reunification plan, while keeping sanctions authority and counterterrorism cooperation as its main levers. Washington wants a single executive quickly, not a lengthy election process it cannot control.

## Key Actions

- **2025-2026:** Established the Boulos track, offering US oil investment and political support in return for a unified executive.
- **August 2026:** The plan was reported to keep Dbeibah as prime minister and give Saddam Haftar the Presidency Council, with military unification to follow.
- **5 November 2026:** Boulos warned that actors obstructing the transition could be sanctioned, and raised concerns about the Central Bank.
- **November 2026:** Washington pressed both camps to accept the deal, while publicly criticising the eastern ratification of Turkey's maritime memorandum as "provocative".
- **2026:** The Federal Reserve paused interactions with the Central Bank of Libya after Fawzi Abokhzam's appointment, adding financial pressure to the political track.

## Current Position

Washington is the only actor that can simultaneously offer sanctions relief, oil investment and political cover to both camps. The Boulos plan is its attempt to convert that leverage into a single executive without elections. But the plan is a personality bargain, not an institutional settlement, and the US has no force on the ground to guarantee it. Its priority is speed and a stable oil supplier, not the shape of Libya's long-term governance.

## Constraints

- It has no military presence and relies on sanctions and diplomatic pressure.
- The plan is contested by the High Council of State, Libyan parties and European allies who see it as entrenching the Haftar family.
- Competing priorities in the Middle East and Ukraine limit sustained attention.
