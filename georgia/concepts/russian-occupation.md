# Russian Occupation

## Overview

Russia occupies Abkhazia and South Ossetia, about a fifth of Georgia's territory, and has done so since the 2008 war. In Georgian usage "occupation" is a legal category as much as a description: it denies the breakaway governments legitimacy, commits Tbilisi to non-recognition, and turns any proposal that treats the regions as states into a red line. The conflict is frozen, but Russia keeps consolidating control while Georgia tries to keep the issue internationalised.

## What Russia Controls

Moscow maintains military bases in both regions and controls the administrative boundaries. The de facto authorities depend on Russian budgets, security services and passports. In South Ossetia the 2026 leadership change made that dependence explicit: de facto president Alan Gagloev resigned on 23 June and was appointed an adviser to Vladimir Putin, and Marat Kambolov, a Russian citizen and former Kurchatov Institute director, won the snap election with 85%. Russian officials have also circulated a rail corridor through Abkhazia, "Putin's Path", that would turn occupied territory into a transit asset. Georgia says it will not allow through-traffic without control of the border.

## The Non-Recognition Firewall

Only four UN members recognise the territories: Russia, Nicaragua, Venezuela and Syria. Nauru withdrew recognition on 30 July, and the EU is now pressing Syria to do the same as part of its modernised agreement. This firewall is Georgia's main asset: it keeps the status quo from becoming legal. Russia's October demand that Tbilisi guarantee non-use of force and accept border demarcation is aimed at breaking it, because signing would treat the regions as independent states. The Geneva International Discussions remain the formal format, with the new bilateral channel kept separate from it.

## Georgia's Dilemma

Georgian Dream wants trade, transport and visa relief without legitimising the occupation. On 21 October it appointed First Deputy Foreign Minister Giorgi Zurabashvili as the prime minister's special representative for Russia, and on 16 November Moscow confirmed contact, naming Deputy Foreign Minister Mikhail Galuzin as his counterpart. Critics say a serving diplomat in the role edges toward restoring diplomatic relations; Kobakhidze insists full relations cannot be restored while Russia occupies the territories. Russia's October import ban and the sharp fall in Russian money transfers show the limits of accommodation.

## Current Status

The first Zurabashvili-Galuzin meeting is expected within weeks, possibly in a third country, with diplomatic relations off the agenda. Russia shows no sign of withdrawing and continues to treat South Ossetia as a bargaining chip rather than absorb it outright. The channel can produce tactical relief on trade or travel, but not a settlement: Georgia will not recognise the territories, and Russia will not give them up.
