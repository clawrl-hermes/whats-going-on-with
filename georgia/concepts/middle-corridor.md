# The Middle Corridor

## Overview

The Middle Corridor, or Trans-Caspian International Transport Route, links China to Europe through Kazakhstan, the Caspian, Azerbaijan, Georgia and Turkey, bypassing Russia and Iran. For Georgia it is the main economic argument for its geopolitical importance: transit fees, logistics investment and a role in Western supply-chain diversification. Its weakness is that it is multimodal, underbuilt and dependent on several states that do not share a single strategy.

## Structure

The route combines rail, Caspian shipping and road. Cargo crosses the Caspian by ferry or container ship, enters Georgia via Azerbaijan, and reaches Europe through Turkey or the Black Sea. Georgian infrastructure includes the Baku-Tbilisi-Kars railway, the Batumi and Poti ports, the East-West highway and the planned Anaklia deep-water port. The binding constraints are the Caspian crossing, Georgian rail capacity and Turkish border delays; each adds cost and uncertainty that the Northern Corridor does not have.

## Strategic Significance

The corridor's value is political as much as commercial. It gives the West a route that avoids Russia and Iran, gives China a land bridge that avoids the same chokepoints, and gives Georgia leverage with both. Washington has made supply-chain diversification a priority, which partly explains its interest in a reset with Tbilisi. The corridor also has competitors: the TRIPP route through Armenia, the reopening of Turkey-Armenia trade and Russia's own "Putin's Path" proposal all threaten to dilute Georgia's transit monopoly.

## Current Status

The Baku-Tbilisi-Kars railway reopened in June 2026, and volumes have grown quickly from a low base: container traffic rose 71% in January-August 2025 and cargo volume in 2024 was 15 times the 2023 level. China's exit from Anaklia in July removed the corridor's flagship private investor, leaving the port state-led and without a strategic anchor. The EU is weighing rail investment in Nakhchivan and has committed €500 million to Armenia-Georgia power links, while Russia's strategy is to keep the corridor dependent rather than block it. Georgia's transit leverage is real but shrinking as alternative routes multiply.
