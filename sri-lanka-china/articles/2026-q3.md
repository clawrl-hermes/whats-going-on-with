## The short version

Sri Lanka spent the third quarter of 2026 converting an economic recovery into a different financial relationship with China. The debt crisis was largely settled: by August the Treasury reported 94% of public external debt restructuring implemented, eleven creditor-committee agreements worth $4.3 billion concluded, and reserves back to $6.5 billion. What changed was the shape of the relationship. China was no longer financing mega-infrastructure at pre-crisis scale. It was extending a smaller, yuan-denominated loan, sending grants and pushing a free trade agreement. Colombo, meanwhile, was courting Indian investment and trying to keep Washington's tariff preferences.

## A recovery that changed the bargaining

The World Bank reclassified Sri Lanka as an upper-middle-income economy on 1 July, after GNI per capita reached $4,670 and the economy grew 5% in 2025. The symbolism mattered: the country had fallen out of that bracket during the crisis. It also changed the terms of the conversation with Beijing. A recovering borrower with market access is less desperate than a defaulting one, and Sri Lanka's leverage improved as reserves and tax revenue recovered.

The quarter's data confirmed the improvement. The Q2 debt bulletin, published at the end of August, put total public debt at Rs32.977 trillion, or about $98 billion, with the debt-to-GDP ratio down to 88.8% from 95% at the end of 2025. Bilateral debt to China edged up to $5.01 billion from $4.974 billion — a modest rise that reflected interest and exchange-rate effects more than new lending.

## The yuan loan

The most consequential financial event of the quarter was quiet: cabinet approval for a $500 million loan from China Exim Bank denominated in renminbi, to finish Section I of the Central Expressway. It is Sri Lanka's first yuan-denominated bilateral loan.

The terms tell the story of the post-restructuring relationship. The original 2019 credit was $989 million; only $51.5 million had been disbursed before the crisis suspended the project. Exim Bank cut its commitment to $500 million, and Sri Lanka must fund $438 million from its own coffers while settling $200 million in outstanding claims to the Chinese contractor MCC. Colombo has no renminbi income and will have to convert dollar reserves to repay.

The loan is also the first concrete implementation of a lending shift AidData reported in June: Exim Bank is encouraging, and sometimes requiring, sovereign borrowers to move from dollars to renminbi. For Beijing, that advances the internationalisation of its currency. For Colombo, it lowers the headline interest rate but adds currency risk. Five Sri Lankan construction firms urged competitive tenders; the government kept MCC.

## Ports, grants and the new model

Hambantota carried the quarter's clearest commercial signal. Container volumes kept climbing after MSC began switching transshipment calls from Colombo, and China Merchants Port Group said in September that it was studying a capacity expansion. MSC's proposed stake in the port, first reported in July, remained unresolved. A port long cited as the symbol of debt-trap diplomacy was becoming a working container hub.

Beijing also changed instruments. In August it extended an RMB79 million grant — about $11 million, with no repayment obligation — to rebuild 14 bridges destroyed by Cyclone Ditwah, and in September the two governments signed the implementation agreement. The shift from loans to grants is small in dollar terms but significant in kind. It suggests Beijing wants to keep a visible presence in Sri Lanka without adding to a debt stock it has just restructured.

## India's opening

India's role expanded across the same months. Mazagon Dock's 51% acquisition of Colombo Dockyard was completed in April, Adani already operates the West Container Terminal, and India, the UAE and Sri Lanka are developing Trincomalee as an energy hub. India provided about $4 billion in emergency financing during the crisis and $450 million in Cyclone Ditwah relief.

The contrast in development footprints is stark. China's capital is concentrated in the south — Hambantota, Port City, the expressways, the coal plant and Mattala. India's assistance is more dispersed and people-centred in the north. That geography shapes politics: the south is the Rajapaksa heartland, while the north is not.

Colombo also signalled distance from Beijing where it could. It refused entry to the Chinese research vessel Shi Yan 6 in March and paused Chinese naval visits indefinitely.

## The FTA question

China spent the quarter pushing a free trade agreement that has been stalled since 2017. Ambassador Qi Zhenhong used his July farewell to renew the call; his successor, Wei Huaxiang, arrived in August and picked up the same theme. Both sides have tied momentum to 2027, the 70th anniversary of diplomatic relations and the 75th of the Rubber-Rice Pact.

The economics are real but awkward. Sri Lanka's trade deficit exceeded $6 billion in 2024, and a World Bank simulation found an FTA could raise exports by 24.2%. But Sri Lankan manufacturers fear cheaper Chinese goods. The government wants market access without exposing its smaller industrial base — the same bargain that has kept negotiations alive without concluding them.

## What to watch

The next tests are concrete. The IMF's seventh review was expected in the autumn and had not concluded by the end of September; the programme expires in March 2027, and a successor arrangement is the backdrop for everything else. The Central Expressway loan has to be disbursed and repaid in a currency Sri Lanka does not earn. MSC's stake in Hambantota could change the port's ownership and commercial logic. And the FTA will either move in 2027 or become another anniversary gesture.

The larger question is whether the shift from infrastructure loans to trade, grants and renminbi credit is a durable new model or a pause while both sides wait to see how quickly Sri Lanka recovers.
