# The Debt-Trap Diplomacy Debate

## Overview

"Debt-trap diplomacy" is the claim that China deliberately lends to countries for unviable projects, expects default, and then seizes strategic assets. Sri Lanka and Hambantota are its most cited example. The evidence is more ambiguous than the phrase suggests, and the debate is as much political as economic.

## The Claim

The narrative rests on four propositions: Chinese loans were large relative to Sri Lanka's repayment capacity; the projects were not commercially viable; the 2017 Hambantota lease transferred a strategic asset; and the lease was a debt-for-equity swap forced by Beijing. The story was popularised by the New York Times in 2018 and promoted by US officials as evidence of Chinese predation.

## What the Record Shows

The record contradicts the strongest version. The Hambantota loan agreements contained no asset-seizure clauses and did not use the port as collateral. Sri Lanka sought Chinese financing after India and Japan declined, and it initiated the 2016 lease after both again declined. China Merchants paid $1.12 billion for its 70% stake, above the port's valuation, and the loan debt was not cancelled. At the time of the lease, China held about 9% of Sri Lanka's government external debt. Most Chinese loans were concessional, averaging around 3.2%, cheaper than the international bonds at 6.9%.

## Why the Narrative Persists

The lease is still a 99-year transfer of a strategic port, and China was slow to restructure during the crisis. The crisis itself was multi-causal: tax cuts, lost tourism, high-interest commercial borrowing and the pandemic mattered more than Chinese lending. But Hambantota became a useful symbol for creditors, opposition parties and rival powers, and symbols do not require precise evidence.

## Current Status

The debate has shifted from seizure to governance. Hambantota is now a functioning container port, the debt has been restructured, and the live questions are who operates the asset, on what terms, and whether the revenue serves the public. That is a less dramatic argument than "debt trap", but a more accurate one.
